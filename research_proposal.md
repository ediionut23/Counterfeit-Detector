## 1. Introduction and Motivation

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are neural networks that work on graphs — data made of objects and the connections between them (Scarselli et al., 2008). Molecules are a natural example: atoms are the objects, chemical bonds the connections. A GNN describes each atom with a vector, then repeatedly lets neighbouring atoms exchange information, so that each atom's vector comes to reflect the part of the molecule around it; these vectors are combined into one description of the molecule and used to make a prediction. In chemistry this is normally used for molecular property prediction — estimating a quantity such as toxicity or solubility from structure alone.

Counterfeit medicines are a serious target for this technology. The World Health Organization estimates that at least one in ten medical products in low- and middle-income countries is substandard or falsified, at a cost of some thirty billion dollars a year. Detection today is done in laboratories: Raman spectroscopy, hyperspectral imaging and image classifiers over pills and packaging all analyse the physical product, so a suspect item must first be found, seized and transported. Detecting a counterfeit from its chemical structure would instead allow screening wherever a structure is known.

The task fits GNNs, since counterfeits are structural by construction: an authentic drug with a small chemistry-aware modification, such as a bioisosteric substitution or a halogen swap. Two things make it hard. The signal is local: the two classes share almost every aggregate property and differ only in one small region, while a GNN compresses the whole molecule into a single vector, in which a difference of one atom among forty is easily lost. And a GNN is a black box: after several rounds of aggregation the original structure is no longer visible in its final representation, so a verdict arrives with no indication of the fragment responsible — of little use to an inspector and of none as evidence. Explainability methods exist, but an attribution that looks reasonable need not be faithful; here it can at least be checked, since counterfeits generated by known transformations come with their modified atoms known in advance.

## 2. State of the Art and Impact

Work on pharmaceutical counterfeit detection relies almost entirely on physical measurements taken from a tablet or its packaging. Raman spectroscopy — which records the optical fingerprint of a sample — is the most common, ranging from direct matching against a reference spectrum to portable field devices and machine-learning classifiers trained on the recorded spectra (Liu et al., 2017). Complementary modalities include hyperspectral imaging and convolutional models applied to photographs of packaging or pills (Larios Delgado et al., 2019). All of these require a physical sample or a photograph of one, and classify the object rather than the chemical structure behind it. None has produced a public dataset against which a structural method could be compared.

Representing molecules as graphs and training neural networks directly on them has become a paradigm of its own, but for a different task. The Message Passing Neural Network framework unified earlier graph-based proposals (Gilmer et al., 2017) and was later extended with richer chemical priors and with heterogeneous formulations distinguishing atom and bond types. These methods all predict a property of the whole molecule from the entire graph, and the standard benchmarks are built for that setting, with labels obtained from physical measurement. MoleculeNet (Wu et al., 2018) gathers seventeen such datasets and introduced scaffold splits, which place molecules sharing a common core on the same side of the split, so that a model is tested on cores it has not seen; the Open Graph Benchmark (Hu et al., 2020) later turned this into a unified protocol — fixed splits, prescribed metrics and public leaderboards — and made molecular graphs one of its standard graph-level tasks.

Counterfeit detection differs from that setting in three ways, and the third is the reason this proposal is about a benchmark. The label is binary rather than continuous. The signal separating the classes sits in a small region of the molecule rather than in the molecule as a whole. And, most importantly, property prediction targets something that exists in the world: toxicity is a fact about a compound, measurable in a laboratory. "Counterfeit" is not a property of a molecule — it is an ordinary molecule that we interpret as counterfeit relative to the drug it imitates, and the same structure could be a legitimate analog in another context. The label is assigned rather than measured: a molecule counts as counterfeit because it was derived from a known parent through a known transformation, which makes the choice of transformations as important as the choice of model.

Graph-level anomaly detection is closer in spirit. Such methods learn what a normal graph looks like — by reconstructing normal instances, or by encoding them contrastively — and score a new graph by how badly it fits (Ding et al., 2019). What they detect is a graph that stands out as a whole: unusual in size, in connectivity, or in the mix of node types it contains. A counterfeit is none of these. It changes one small part of an authentic drug and leaves the rest intact, so it shares all of those properties with the compound it imitates. Nothing about it is unusual; only one region is different — which is what these methods are not built to notice.

Since no benchmark exists for this task, its design has to be borrowed from neighbouring ones, where several precedents are instructive. Errica et al. (2020) contributed no data at all, but a protocol: once model selection was standardized for graph classification, several published gains disappeared, and simple structure-agnostic baselines remained competitive. Lv et al. (2021) found the same for heterogeneous GNNs — their reported advantage shrank once every baseline was tuned under equal conditions. DUD-E (Mysinger et al., 2012), a virtual-screening benchmark, matched its negatives to its positives on physicochemical properties so that no classifier could separate the two on molecular weight alone; this is the protocol already adopted for the first dataset. Sanchez-Lengeling et al. (2020) built molecular classification tasks whose correct attribution is computable in advance, and GraphXAI (Agarwal et al., 2023) generates graphs together with the explanations that are true for them; both turn explainability from something demonstrated into something measured — which counterfeit detection allows for free, since the transformation applied, and therefore the atom modified, is known. The closest analog is MoleculeACE (van Tilborg et al., 2022), which isolates activity cliffs, pairs of molecules that are nearly identical in structure yet differ sharply in potency: across more than twenty methods, descriptor-based models beat the deep ones on exactly those pairs — a warning that a graph network is not automatically the right tool when the signal is a small local edit.

Structure-based counterfeit detection therefore rests on a single paper, submitted by the candidate to IEEE DSAA (a B-class conference, currently under review), together with a first dataset of fifty thousand molecular graphs, released publicly (Tanasă et al., 2026). That work establishes the framing and gives the area its starting point; turning it into a benchmark requires a wider range of transformations and a larger scale, so that results obtained on it transfer beyond it. What is missing is not availability but scope: a family of datasets covering the range of modifications, a scale at which results transfer, an agreed protocol, and a baseline suite covering more than two hand-tuned models. The impact would be twofold. Results in this area would become comparable for the first time. And because the benchmark supplies atom-level ground truth for the discriminative substructure, it would give the graph learning community a realistic task on which methods that detect or explain local structure can be measured rather than only demonstrated.

## 3. Proposed Contributions

**C1. The dataset component of the benchmark.** A benchmark is a task definition together with data, fixed splits, an evaluation protocol, metrics and a baseline suite; this contribution concerns its data component, while the protocol and the baselines are addressed in C2 and C4. That component should not be a single dataset. The proposal is to release several versions, each built to answer a different question.

The first division is by type of modification. Rather than pooling every counterfeit into one set, the benchmark would provide a separate dataset per transformation category, and per subcategory wherever the chemistry supports the distinction. Pooling hides what a practitioner needs to know: a single averaged F1 says a model is good, not that it recognizes halogen swaps almost perfectly and misses scaffold hops entirely. Reported per category, the results answer the question in the form it is actually asked — which model detects which kind of modification. The catalogue itself would grow from twenty-one rules to roughly a hundred, so each category holds enough examples for that comparison to mean something.

The second division is by how counterfeits are generated. The dataset built so far applies a fixed catalogue of transformation rules taken from the medicinal-chemistry literature. Such a list has a ceiling: it produces only the modifications somebody thought to write down, whereas a real counterfeiter is not restricted to a published catalogue. The benchmark would therefore also include versions in which the modifications are not specified in advance but discovered — obtained through an evolutionary search over molecular structures, in which candidates are evolved against criteria such as similarity to the original drug and plausibility as a compound, so useful ones survive and the rest are discarded. Comparing models across rule-based and evolved versions then answers what no single version can: whether a detector trained on the modifications we know about holds up against modifications nobody enumerated.

The third division is by dataset properties, constructing versions that differ deliberately in one characteristic at a time; their purpose is described in C4. Across all versions, each molecule ships with three-dimensional conformers, and each counterfeit with the atoms its transformation modified, so explanations can be scored against the true edit using explanation accuracy, Fidelity and Sparsity, along the axes established by Sanchez-Lengeling et al. (2020) and Agarwal et al. (2023).

**C2. Comparative study of architectures.** Only two models have been tried so far, both tuned by hand, which is not enough to conclude anything. The comparison would be extended in three tiers, so that the task is bracketed from below and from above rather than sampled only in the middle.

Below sit simpler, non-GNN baselines: Random Forest and XGBoost over Morgan fingerprints. They answer whether a graph network is justified at all — a real question here, since a fingerprint baseline has been reported to outperform GIN and GraphSAGE on three of four molecular datasets (Errica et al., 2020), and descriptor models beat deep ones on activity cliffs (van Tilborg et al., 2022), and a counterfeit differs from its parent precisely by the presence of a substructure, which is what a fingerprint encodes. In the middle sit the graph networks themselves: GCN, GAT, GIN and heterogeneous formulations, together with graph transformers: attention over every pair of atoms is message passing on a densely connected graph, so these are GNNs rather than a separate family, and whether restricting which atoms may exchange information is a useful prior or a cost is a question within this tier. Above sit molecular transformers pretrained over large SMILES corpora and then fine-tuned here: they bring knowledge from millions of molecules that no model trained on this benchmark alone possesses. If pretraining at that scale transfers to a task defined by a local edit, the benchmark should show it; if it does not, that is itself worth reporting.

Every model would be tuned under an equal Bayesian hyperparameter optimization budget and averaged over several seeds, without which a difference between models cannot be separated from a difference in tuning effort — the failure mode documented by Errica et al. (2020) and Lv et al. (2021). Optuna and Ax are both suitable; which one fits better is a practical question for the work itself.

**C3. Study of molecular representations.** An investigation of how the representation of a molecule affects performance. The study would measure the effect of enriching atom embeddings with additional structural information: ring membership and ring size, membership in chemical motifs, positional encodings (random-walk or Laplacian), geometric features derived from the three-dimensional conformers, and other topological properties such as centrality. The aim is to identify which information contributes most to detecting local structural modifications — how much of the task can be solved by describing the molecule better rather than by making the model larger.

**C4. Performance Analysis across Factors.** A single averaged score answers almost nothing. This contribution analyses what governs performance, across the three factors C1 varies independently, measured throughout with False Negative Rate first, then Precision, Recall and F1.

The first is the type of modification: every result is reported per transformation category, and separately for the rule-based and evolved versions, rather than pooled. The second is class imbalance: counterfeits are rare in any screening setting, so ratios of 1:10, 1:100 and 1:1000 quantify how performance degrades as they become scarce, with graph-size imbalance varied alongside it.

The third is the properties of the dataset itself, reported to govern GNN performance as much as the architecture does. Some are chemical and transfer directly: scaffold diversity above all, since models overfit to the scaffolds seen in training and degrade on unfamiliar cores, which is why scaffold-based splits are consistently harder than random ones (Wu et al., 2018; Hu et al., 2020). Others are graph-theoretic and transfer uncertainly: degree distribution, edge homogeneity and within-class feature similarity are influential across models (Li et al., 2023), but that work concerns node-level tasks on single large graphs. Graph-level analyses do exist — Errica et al. (2020) on how the choice of dataset reorders graph classifiers, van Tilborg et al. (2022) on activity-cliff density — but each covers one property at a time, so whether the broader catalogue carries over to classification over many small graphs is open rather than settled, and this proposal is in a position to test it.

The analysis therefore runs in both directions: which known properties still matter here, and which properties specific to this task matter but appear nowhere in that literature — similarity between a counterfeit and its parent, the size of the edit relative to the molecule, whether it falls in the scaffold or the periphery. Because the data is generated rather than collected, each factor can be varied while the rest are held fixed, which no observational study can do. Any property found to matter tells whoever builds the next dataset what to control.

## 4. Conclusions and Future Work

The outcome would be a public benchmark for counterfeit medicine detection with Graph Neural Networks: a family of datasets, a standardized evaluation protocol, and an experimental analysis of the factors that influence performance, yielding recommendations on the choice of architecture, of molecular representation and of evaluation strategy under realistic conditions — the first questions anyone building on the benchmark faces. The work would be carried out in Python using PyTorch Geometric.

A benchmark is only used if using it is easy, and only trusted if every number in it can be re-derived. Everything the work produces would therefore be released: the datasets, together with a specification of the input and output formats; the fixed splits; the training and evaluation procedures, as documented, publicly archived code written to be read rather than only run; the software and hardware specifications and a versioned environment, so that a reported result can be reproduced rather than taken on trust; and the trained models themselves, with the procedures needed to load and apply them. That last point matters more than it appears: releasing weights is what makes a competing method cheap to compare against, and a benchmark others choose to compare against is the only kind that has any effect.

A natural next direction is multimodal detection: combining molecular structure with Raman spectroscopy or hyperspectral imaging, where a structural prior able to compensate for the noise of a low-cost portable spectrometer would point towards inexpensive field screening that neither modality supports on its own.
